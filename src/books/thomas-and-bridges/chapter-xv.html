Chapter XV: Joseph Bridger of England

The American ancestral line of the Bridges family of Trigg County, Kentucky, dates back to the early 1650’s, when Joseph Bridger of Gloucester, England, arrived in Virginia.1

Joseph Bridger was one among a group of Royalists who remained loyal to the King of England despite the Civil War being waged there under the guidance of Oliver Cromwell. Thus, by 1653 when Cromwell became Lord Protector, many of the Royalists fled to Virginia, Joseph Bridger among them.

Actually the exact date of Joseph’s arrival in Virginia is not known, but if 1653 was the year, he was 25 years old at the time. In any event, his name first appeared in the official records of Virginia in 1657 when he was listed as a member of the House of Burgesses. He also served as a member of that body the following year and again in 1663.2

Joseph Bridger was a man of many talents and abilities. With the help of his father-in-law, Colonel Robert Pitt, and a man named William Burgh, he set out to obtain land in the new world, ultimately acquiring several thousand acres of valuable real estate in Isle of Wight County. The first land transaction in which Joseph was involved was recorded on March 21, 1664 when he, with the two other men mentioned, patented 3,000 acres of land located on a branch of the Blackwater River. Joseph’s portion was claimed through his having transported 24 persons to the colony, among them, William Thomas, an early-day member of a branch of the Thomas family. Two years later, June 7, 1666, Joseph Bridger, then called Captain Bridger, and William Burgh, patented 7,800 acres of land in Isle of Wight County “. . .beginning by a White Marsh, a meadow about halfe a mile from the main run of the Blackwater. . .” for transportation of 156 persons. The list of headrights included: William Thomas (likely the same one used in his first patent); Edward Thomas, Thomas Pitt, Joseph’s brother-in-law; James Bridger, his brother, and Joseph himself.3

The Blackwater River is a stream that originates near Petersburg in Prince George County, Virginia. It runs generally southeast to south and in many places it is the dividing line between Surry and Sussex counties; Southampton, Isle of Wight and Nansemond counties. It runs some 45 or 50 miles through the productive lowlands of Virginia’s Tidewater country to a confluence with the Nottaway River at the state line. At that point a combination of the two streams forms the Chowan River that runs south across North Carolina and widens into Albemarle Sound.

The land along the Blackwater was dark and fertile, ideal for the growing of tobacco, and Joseph Bridger built his plantation house on an 850 acre site which he had bought from Captain Upton, using bricks made by hand and baked on the place. He eventually acquired additional land — in all, over 12,000 acres — and soon put it to work raising tobacco and cattle. In due time Joseph Bridger was shipping his products to England by the ship load.

Successful in business and in the political life, Joseph Bridger rapidly rose to prominence in Isle of Wight County and the Virginia colony. He was called on in 1664 to serve as a member of the Commission appointed to adjust the boundary line between the states of Virginia and Maryland. Two years later, he was named adjutant general of the Virginia forces while also serving as a member of the general assembly. That same year he was one of the commissioners representing Virginia who conferred with a similar group from Maryland and North Carolina relative to the tobacco interests of planters in those states. As one of Virginia’s leading tobacco growers, he had a keen interest in this conference.

Joseph Bridger married Hester Pitt, daughter of Col. Pitt, his original land patenting partner prior to coming to Virginia. The Pitt family had also lived in Gloucester, England, near where the Bridger family lived, and there is evidence of intermarriage of the two families in England. Joseph and Hester (Pitt) Bridger settled in the big plantation house in Isle of Wight County and raised their family, four girls and three boys.

Despite this domestic setting however, Joseph took time out when the Indian War broke in 1675 to assume command of the militia from Isle of Wight County, all the while still serving on the council.4

The year 1676 was a significant one as far as the Bridger family was concerned, for it marked the beginning of a rift between Joseph Bridger and his oldest son, Joseph Jr., that eventually was to alienate them completely.

The difficulty began with what is known as Bacon’s Rebellion. Led by 29-year-old Nathaniel Bacon, a Virginia colonial politician and soldier, the Rebellion centered around the economic exploitation of the small farmers who lived in outlying districts scattered along an unprotected frontier far back from the shores of the James River. Despite these vicious and bloody attacks, during which time many settlers were wiped out, Governor William Berkeley had refused assistance, being willing, he was charged, that the border population should suffer while he and his adherents enjoyed a very profitable fur trade with the same Indians.

Nathaniel Bacon, though from a wealthy family himself, took up the cause of the farmers, commanded two unauthorized but successful expeditions against the Indians, one of them at Roanoke Island in North Carolina. After those encounters, Bacon went to Jamestown in June 1676 as the representative of Henrico, his home county, in the newly elected House of Burgesses which the governor had been forced to convene. Bacon brought many supporters to the capital, among them Joseph Bridger, Jr., and forced Berkeley into passing laws that would benefit the small land owners, and at the same time granting him a commission to continue his Indian campaigns.

The governor, seeing that he was going to have to face a battle with Bacon, fled to his home, “Green Springs,” located on the eastern shore of the James River some two or three miles from what today is the city of Williamsburg. Fleeing with the governor was Joseph Bridger, Sr., denounced by Bacon along with the governor and many other prominent Virginians. However, before he left, Joseph is reported to have buried a goodly portion of his money at White Marsh to prevent it from falling into the hands of Bacon while he was gone. It probably was a wise move for during Joseph’s absence, his plantation was plundered and his cattle and other property taken.

Bacon and Berkeley clashed a second time, with the governor coming out second best again, so he fled once more to Green Spring. Bacon, angered, set fire to Jamestown, burning the public buildings and all the valuable records of the activities of the colony up to that time. Unfortunately, Bacon was stricken, some say of malaria, others say of dysentery, and died October 1, 1676, before he could carry out the reforms he had promised the small farmers. Without a leader, the Rebellion collapsed. After a few months, Governor Berkeley returned, captured all of Bacon’s men he could find, and hanged a number of them before he was forced to return to England.

Sir John Berry, one of the commissioners sent to Virginia by Charles II to report on the progress of Governor Berkeley, had this to say about Joseph Bridger: “(He was) a very resolute gentleman, who though forced to flee in the heat of war in his own countrie, yet on his return, was very active and instrumental in reducing to their obedience the South parte of the James River.”

Joseph Bridger, in his allegience to King Charles II, remained at Green Spring with Governor Berkeley, and while there, witnessed the governor’s will. He also was a member of the governor’s court which condemned to death the leaders of Bacon’s Rebellion. It was through his influence, no doubt, that his son, Joseph Jr., was pardoned for the part he played in the Rebellion and was permitted to go free. Despite this apparent act of fatherly love however, Joseph Sr., never forgave his son and disinherited him in his will.

In 1680, reminescent of Nathaniel Bacon, Joseph Bridger, Sr., was commissioned Commander in chief of the militia and ordered to raise men to protect the frontier against the Indians in an area that included not only Isle of Wight County, but also Surry, Lower Norfolk and Upper Norfolk (later Nansemond) counties.5 His title was major general, and eventually, in his later years, he was known throughout the area as General Bridger. He continued his close association with the governor and became a dominant figure in Virginia politics during the last years of his life. One of his last official duties was in 1683 when he served as a member of the council of state and of the general court along with 10 other men, many of whose descendants in years to come would be prominent in charting the progress of the State of Virginia.

THE OLD BRICK CHURCH

No account of the life and times of Joseph Bridger can be written without devoting some attention to the Old Brick Church in Isle of Wight County, near Smithfield, Virginia. Known as St.Luke’s, this structure today is said to be the oldest church in the nation.

The traditional builder was for many years believed to be Joseph Bridger’s father. According to Miss Elizabeth Jordan,6 secretary of Historic St. Luke’s Restoration, when Joseph Bridger came to Virginia in the 1650’s, he is said to have brought with him a letter from the Bishop of London directing him, when he arrived in Virginia, to look after the Brick Church of Corawoak (sometimes spelled Warrasquyoake and other ways). Miss Jordan said that many years ago, Mrs. Walter Dey of Norfolk, Virginia, told her and her sisters that her Grandfather Bridger showed her the letter when she was a child. Unfortunately the letter was burned when her grandfather’s house in Portsmouth, Virginia, was destroyed by fire.

As for the traditional builder being Joseph Bridger, father of Joseph of Virginia, this seems to be in error. John Smythe, who came to Virginia from England and settled in the Berkley Hundred, wrote a history of the Berkley Hundred of Gloucester, England. This account was published in three large volumes and in it he minutely described the life and times at Slimbridge and other areas of Gloucester. One of the families he wrote about was that of Lawrence Bridger, grandfather of Joseph of Virginia, who was rector at Slimbridge.

According to Smythe, the father of Joseph Bridger who came to Virginia was named Samuel Bridger. In the 1930’s this reference was used as a basis for a Bridger family sketch in the book “Seventeenth Century Isle of Wight County,” by John Bennett Boddie. However, in support of the tradition that the structure was built by one of the Bridger family, it is now believed by authorities of the Historic St. Luke’s Restoration, that Joseph Bridger, a half brother of Samuel, the father of Joseph of Virginia, was the architect of the building. Miss Jordan said this was a strong possibility but “. . .we have not been able to locate any documentary proof. We wrote the Rector of the church at Slimbridge in an attempt to get accurate information, but he wrote saying that Smythe’s book was not considered reliable there.”

As for proof of the date of construction of the building being 1632, one has to go back to 1887. That year a severe storm blew down a large tree which fell on the southeast corner of the old building and damaged many of the original bricks. When the rubble had been cleared three bricks were found with the date 1632 pressed into them. Two of those bricks were broken, but the third one, sound as the day it was made, was put back into the restored wall and it can be seen today under the east window behind the altar.

Despite the rent in the curtain of family tradition that Joseph Bridger’s father built the Old Brick Church, it is definite that many members of the Bridger family did worship there and were closely affiliated with it. A number of Joseph’s sons as well as several of his grandsons, served as members of the vestry there in the ensuing years.

Today the Old Brick Church located four miles south of Smithfield, Virginia, on State Road 10, still stands and it is open daily to the public. The attractive and charming old structure has been described as “. . .the most precious building in America,” and remains one of the most continuing symbols of the history of the development of our nation. Its picturesque interior captures the setting of Angelican worship, and the famed communion table and chairs are among the rarest such works created by early craftsmen. The 17th century American silver baptisimal basin sets in a font hewn from a great log that has been carefully shaped by hand. On display also is an Elizabethan organ, lent by the Queen of England.

Importance of the Old Brick Church as a legacy was summed up by President Dwight D. Eisenhower in a letter to St. Luke’s October 12, 1956, in which he said:

“This monument to the founders of our country is in truth a national shrine. Visitors there, inescapably, will be reminded of the deep religious convictions of the first settlers, their faith in God and their faith in themselves as children of God. St. Luke’s, more than a time-hallowed relic of the past, will be an enduring witness to the spirit that animates them, for within its walls our forefathers gained new courage, fine persevernace, abiding strength to make of the wilderness a home for themselves and all who followed them.”

Joseph Bridger, probably one of the wealthiest and most influential men of his time, died on April 15, 1686. He left a very large amount of personal property and approximately 12,000 acres of land in Isle of Wight county. In addition, he also owned land in Surry and James City counties of Virginia and considerable acreage in the state of Maryland.

He was buried in a field near his home, White Marsh, located less than three miles from the Old Brick Church. A marble slab was erected at his grave and bore this inscription:

SACRED

To ye memory of The Honorable Joseph Bridger Esq. Councel of state in Virginia To King Charles Ye 2nd Dying April ye 15: A:D: 1686 Aged 58 years mournfully left His wife 3 sons and 4 daughters Does nature silent mourn & can dumb stone Make his true worth to future ages knowne Excels expression marble sure will keep His mem’ry best yet ever on his grave shall weep Here lies ye late great minister of State That Royal virtues had & Royal fate To Charles his Counsels did such honors bring His own express fetched him t’attend ye king His soul yet ever did with vigor move Nimble took wing, soared like it selfe above For ye bright stars ner’s laysily decline But in an instant shoot yet cease to shine.7

In the passing of time, White Marsh, the big plantation house, was occupied by various descendants of Joseph Bridger. However, it eventually fell into disrepair and was demolished, its handmade bricks scattered in the nearby field.

In 1887-1894, during the restoration of St. Luke’s church, it was discovered that only two gravestones were left in the old Bridger family cemetery at White Marsh. These two slabs marked the graves of Joseph Bridger and of Ann Randall, the latter believed to have been the aunt by marriage of Joseph’s wife, Hester (Pitt) Bridger. No other graves were identified. However, the remains of the two were reinterred in the chancel of the Old Brick Church, and the marble slab, which had stood at the head of Joseph’s grave for over 200 years, was placed over his tomb, as was that of Ann Randall. Today these two markers are part of the chancel floor and the inscriptions are still fairly legible.

Joseph is the only one of our early ancestors, Bridges or Thomas, whose grave I have been able to locate.

Joseph Bridger made his will August 3, 1683, and attached a codicil less than two months later that disinherited his oldest son, Joseph Jr., apparently for the part he played in Bacon’s Rebellion.

Most of the children of Joseph and Hester (Pitt) Bridger married and settled down in Isle of Wight County, largely on the property they had inherited from their father when he died.

They were:

  1. Martha — married Thomas Goodwin of Nansemond County.
  2. Mary—married Richard Tibboth, captain of the ship “Mary and Anne.”
  3. Elizabeth—married Thomas Lear of Nansemond County.
  4. Hester—married George Williamson of Isle of Wight County.
  5. Samuel — married Mrs. Elizabeth Woory, widow of Joseph Woory in 1683.
  6. William—married Mrs. Elizabeth (Allen) Caufield of Surry County in 1727.
  7. Joseph Jr. - married Elizabeth Norsworthy. The Kentucky Bridges line descends through him.

In order to settle the estate of Joseph Bridger Sr., an inventory was made of his property. Items included in this inventory indicate that Joseph Bridger was one of the wealthiest men in Isle of Wight County. In describing the house, the inventory read: “four rooms on the first floor, including the children’s chamber and the dining room, with two rooms in an upper story.” Also a “new house” is listed in which there was a hall, parlor and the lower chamber on the first floor, and on the second floor three rooms and a “gallery” or hall. All rooms and the halls in both houses were fully furnished. The kitchen with two additional rooms was apparently separated from the main house as was the custom in those days.

Joseph Bridger also operated a mercantile business in a storehouse nearby. This building contained an outer room, a supply room in the rear and a storeroom above. In addition, there was a brick warehouse with storage space on the second floor. Merchandise brought from England was unloaded at the landing on the James River and the inventory listed 800 “painting tiles” among other items stored there.

The inventory also listed a sloop with a capacity of 28 hogsheads, apparently used for shipping tobacco. It was equipped with “furniture, sails, rigging and ground tackle,” obviously ready to sail at a moment’s notice. Tobacco was picked up at the planters’ own wharf after the sloop had been unloaded of its goods from England. Joseph’s own agents, Micajah Perry and Thomas Lane were in charge of his shipping operations.

A cattle raiser as well as a tobacco grower, Joseph had the following livestock to dispose of at his death: 54 head of cattle, seven calves, 13 cows and five yearlings for dairy purposes, eight oxen for doing the heavy work, nine steers and four bulls; 54 old hogs, young hogs, sows, shoats and pigs, seven sheep and 14 horses.

Among Joseph’s personal property was 490 ounces of silver, sixty pounds of Spanish money and 42 pounds ten shillings of English money.

Several people owed him money at the time of his death including: Colonel William Byrd, 12 pounds; John Pleasants, five pounds; John Champion, 958 pounds of tobacco; Thomas Pitt, his brother-in-law, 2,000 pounds of tobacco; Colonel Christopher Wormeley in a bill of exchange totaling eight pounds, and Perry & Lane, London, bills of exchange to Joseph’s credit totaling 654 pounds.

Help in running the house included four indentured servants with existing terms of service (seven years was the usual limit) and 13 Negroes including two small children.

Household items listed in the inventory included: numerous brass and iron andirons, tongs and fire shovels; candlesticks, of brass, wire and silver; chairs caned, leather covered, and couches upholstered with serge or turkey-work; assorted tables, a great looking glass, a number of chests, chests of drawers and numerous pictures.

Other furnishings included bedsteads with feather bedding, bolster and pillows, some with valences and curtains, blankets, sheets and quilts. There was carpeting in the “great hall” of the new house, also a clock and three sets of scales.

A generous supply of napkins and table linen was listed in the dining room as well as pewter tableware including some four dozen plates, a porriger, chafing dish, dish plates and pie plates. Silver pieces included a punch bowl, candlesticks, serving dish, several spoons and a tobacco box cover.

Among the volumes in Joseph’s library were books on witchcraft and astrology.

In addition to his usual possession of firearms for family protection, Joseph Bridger, as a high ranking officer in the Isle of Wight militia left several hand guns, a case of pistols and holsters, a pair of pocket pistols, a cutlass, three rapiers, one with a hilt of silver, and a stock of ammunition.

Clothing included a parcel of Virginia-made cloth and 14 pairs of Virginia stockings, probably woven on the plantation.

Apparently the Bridger household was well fed as the kitchen contained a very large fireplace at one end of the room, and equipment included two great copper kettles one of which weighed 61 pounds; four pot hooks, a heavy mall, three spits and skillets of various sizes, and in the adjoining milk room were 11 milk pails, earthen pans and earthen butter pots.

Last but not least was the cellar where the “lord of the manor” kept his liquor supply. This included cider for family use, a cask of brandy, a cask of old whiskey and a worn-out malt mill.

The inventory of the Bridger store was quite a revelation, particularly as to the variety of items carried for sale in the 17th century. Wearing apparel, accessories of dress, utensils and farming tools, most of which was imported from England, dominated the list. Nearly a score of different fabrics were available in quantities for making clothing, sheeting and other uses. Also on the shelves of the Bridger store was calico, flannel, broadcloth and silk, and knitting needles, pins, combs, knives and scissors. Edging, assorted colored tapes and buttons, ready for the trimming of garments, was in large supply as was soap.

Building supplies—nails, hoes, axes and whipsaws were listed in abundance, and home furnishings included feather ticking, rugs, blankets, curtains, 55 bushels of salt and a barrel of coarse sugar.

Wearing apparel ready made included shoes for men, women and boys, also hose among which were four pair “moth-eaten.”

Horse collars, harness, bridles, bits, halters, girths and saddles rounded out the inventory, all except the stock of drink. The Bridger store’s inventory listed six barrels and one hogshead of rum — approximately 255 gallons, and 65 gallons for “Colonel Powell’s hands.”9

No estimate was made as to the value of the complete estate, but obviously it was quite large. It was divided according to the will, and the shares in the estate of General Bridger’s three married daughters were claimed by their husbands, as was the custom in those days, and are recorded by items. Descendants of his children continued to live in Isle of Wight County, largely on the estate he had amassed during his more than 30 years of life in Virginia.
